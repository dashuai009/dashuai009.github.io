Consider a [puzzle shared by @sphere_homotopy on X](https://x.com/sphere_homotopy/status/2126008331504681043): write down the sequence $1^{17}+9,\ 2^{17}+9,\ 3^{17}+9,\ldots$. The first two terms are $10$ and $131081$, so their gcd is $1$. Is every pair of neighbors coprime? In other words, is it true that

$$
\gcd\!\left(n^{17}+9,\,(n+1)^{17}+9\right)=1
\qquad\text{for all } n\ge 1?
$$

You could test every positive integer with at most 51 digits and never see the pattern break. To find the first failure, turn the question around: **what prime could possibly divide both terms?** Once we know the prime, a linear congruence will tell us where to look.

## Which prime could divide both terms?

Subtract the first term from the second. This leaves the gcd unchanged and gives us two polynomials of different degrees:

$$
F(x)=x^{17}+9,\qquad H(x)=(x+1)^{17}-x^{17}.
$$

If a prime $p$ divides both terms at $n$, then $F(n)\equiv H(n)\equiv 0\pmod p$. To eliminate $n$, we use the *resultant* $R=\operatorname{Res}(F,H)$. The useful fact about $R$ is that it is a fixed integer with an identity

$$
R=A(x)F(x)+B(x)H(x),\qquad A,B\in\mathbb Z[x].
$$

Put $x=n$. Any common divisor of $F(n)$ and $H(n)$ must divide the right-hand side, so it must divide $R$. Instead of checking infinitely many pairs of neighbors, we need the prime factors of just one integer.

### Computing that integer by long division

Work over the rationals and make each polynomial *monic*, meaning its leading coefficient is $1$. Start with $E_{17}=F$ and

$$
E_{16}=\frac{H}{17}=x^{16}+8x^{15}+40x^{14}+\cdots+\frac{1}{17}.
$$

Divide $E_{17}$ by $E_{16}$. The quotient is $x-8$: the $x$ cancels the degree-17 term and the $-8$ cancels the degree-16 term. The remainder starts with $(8\cdot 8-40)x^{15}=24x^{15}$ and ends with $9+8/17=161/17$. Dividing it by $24$ gives a monic $E_{15}$ with constant term $161/408$.

Repeat this division. In this example the degree drops by one each time, so every step has the form

$$
E_{k+1}=(x+u_k)E_k+v_k E_{k-1},
$$

where $v_k$ is the leading coefficient of the remainder before we make it monic. Here are the first three steps:

| $k$ | Quotient $x+u_k$ | Leading coefficient $v_k$ | Constant term $E_{k-1}(0)$ |
| ---: | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 16 | $x-8$ | $24$ | $161/408$ |
| 15 | $x+1/2$ | $19/4$ | $-113/3876$ |
| 14 | $x+1/2$ | $2$ | $793/3876$ |

Why keep the $v_k$'s? At each root of $E_k$, the division identity says that $E_{k+1}$ has the same value as $v_k E_{k-1}$. For a monic first polynomial, the resultant is the product of the second polynomial's values at those roots. There are $k$ roots counted with multiplicity, so this step contributes a factor of $v_k^k$. Swapping the order of two consecutive degrees introduces no minus sign: $k(k+1)$ is even.

Continue down to $E_0=1$. Restoring the factor of $17$ we took out of $H$ gives

$$
R=17^{17}\prod_{k=1}^{16}v_k^{\,k}.
$$

The product is the integer

$$
P=8936582237915716659950962253358945635793453256935559.
$$

It is **prime**. That is a separate arithmetic fact, not something a resultant guarantees; the SageMath check below verifies it. Since the original gcd divides $P$, it can only be $1$ or $P$. We still have to find out when it is $P$.

## Where does that prime occur?

Run the polynomial Euclidean algorithm again, this time with coefficients modulo $P$. Over the rationals the last nonzero remainder was a constant. Modulo $P$ that constant remainder vanishes *before* normalization, so the algorithm stops one step earlier. Its last monic nonzero remainder is the linear polynomial

$$
\gcd_{\mathbb F_P[x]}(F,H)=x+C,\qquad
C=512149312322827330662764931050044963334032796143126.
$$

The constant $C$ comes from the same long division as the table. Set $x=0$ in each division identity to track the constant terms:

$$
E_{k-1}(0)=\frac{E_{k+1}(0)-u_k E_k(0)}{v_k}.
$$

Stop at the linear term $E_1=x+E_1(0)$ and reduce its constant coefficient modulo $P$. The result is $C$.

Now $n$ is a common root modulo $P$ exactly when $x-n$ divides both polynomials, or equivalently when it divides their gcd $x+C$. That leaves just **one residue class**: $n\equiv -C\pmod P$. Since $0<C<P$, its least positive member is

$$
n_0=P-C=8424432925592889329288197322308900672459420460792433.
$$

Substituting $n_0$ proves it is *a* counterexample. The residue class proves it is the *first*: the positive counterexamples are $P-C,\ 2P-C,\ 3P-C,\ldots$. At each one the gcd is exactly $P$; at every integer in between it is $1$.

### Check the arithmetic in SageMath

This computes both the divisor and the input from the polynomials. Neither 52-digit number is an input to the calculation.

```python
from sage.all import GF, ZZ, PolynomialRing, gcd

x = PolynomialRing(ZZ, "x").gen()
F = x**17 + 9
H = (x + 1)**17 - x**17
P = F.resultant(H)
assert P.is_prime(proof=True)

K = GF(P)
d = F.change_ring(K).gcd(H.change_ring(K)).monic()
assert d.degree() == 1
n0 = ZZ(-d[0])  # Least nonnegative representative of the root
assert gcd(F(n0), F(n0 + 1)) == P
print(P, d, n0, sep="\n")
```
